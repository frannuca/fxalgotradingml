\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This document describes, in full mathematical and implementation detail, an FX portfolio
allocation system consisting of two independently-trained neural networks applied
\emph{sequentially}: (1) \textbf{PortfolioLSTM}, which maps a rolling window of multi-asset
FX log returns (optionally augmented with interest-rate carry and vol-normalized momentum
features) to a portfolio weight vector, followed by a volatility-targeting rescaling step;
and (2) \textbf{RiskLSTM}, an optional overlay trained \emph{on top of} the frozen portfolio
decisions, which learns a per-asset attenuation factor in $[\text{max\_attenuation}, 1]$ that
scales down positions when an asset's recent (marginal) and portfolio-wide (cross-sectional)
risk statistics look unstable. Every architectural choice is presented alongside its
rationale, the exact tensor shapes at each stage, the governing mathematical expression, the
source file and line range implementing it, and -- where relevant -- the optional variants
that can be toggled via configuration. Two full architecture diagrams and a data-flow diagram
are included. The document closes with a complete configuration reference and a summary
table of every design decision and its motivation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Notation and Conventions}

Throughout this document, matrix and tensor dimensions are written using the following
symbols, matching the variable names used in code wherever possible:

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{@{}cll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Symbol} & \textbf{Code name} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
\midrule
$B$ & \texttt{batch} / \texttt{n\_samples} & Number of decision samples in a batch (a full training split, in full-batch training) \\
$L$ & \texttt{lookback} & Sequence length: number of trailing days in one input window \\
$N$ & \texttt{n\_assets} & Number of tradable assets (FX pairs, plus \texttt{CASH} if enabled) \\
$C$ & \texttt{n\_channels} & Feature channels per asset (1 = raw return only; $>1$ with carry/vol-norm features) \\
$H$ & \texttt{hidden\_size} & LSTM hidden state width \\
$W$ & \texttt{rolling\_window} (risk) & Trailing window (days) for RiskLSTM's rolling statistics \\
$K$ & \texttt{risk\_rolling\_window}$-1$ & Samples dropped from each split when building RiskLSTM sequences \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A raw daily log-return vector is $r_t \in \mathbb{R}^N$, so an input window for one decision
sample is $X_i \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times NC}$ (flattened as $L \times NC$ in the asset-major
layout described in \S\ref{sec:features}), a full split's worth of input windows is a rank-3
tensor $X \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times L \times NC}$, and the portfolio weight vector for sample
$i$ is $w_i \in \mathbb{R}^N$. Superscript ``raw'' denotes the pre-standardization / pre-vol-targeting
quantity; a hat ($\hat{\cdot}$) denotes a model estimate. All neural network code is
PyTorch; \code{nn.LSTM} is always instantiated with \code{batch\_first=True}, so its own
input/output convention is $(\text{batch}, \text{time}, \text{features})$, matching the
convention used throughout this document.

Every feature described below is a \textbf{configuration flag with a documented default} --
Section~\ref{sec:config} tabulates the full set. Features whose default is \emph{off} are
marked with an \textbf{Optional component} box; enabling them changes the effective network
input/output width, which is always noted explicitly.

\section{System Overview}
% =============================================================================

The system trains two networks \emph{sequentially and independently} -- never jointly.
Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the full data flow, from raw prices to reported PnL.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{Stage \arabic*.}, leftmargin=2.2cm]
  \item \textbf{Data \& features} (\S\ref{sec:data}): daily close prices $\to$ log returns
    $\to$ optional carry / vol-normalized-momentum channels $\to$ rolling windows $\to$
    chronological train/validation/test split $\to$ per-feature standardization.
  \item \textbf{PortfolioLSTM} (\S\ref{sec:portfolio}): maps each window to a raw weight
    vector $\hat w_i^{\text{raw}}$, trained end-to-end (full-batch, over the whole training
    period at once) against a chosen risk-adjusted objective (\S\ref{sec:objectives}), net of
    transaction costs (\S\ref{sec:txcost}).
  \item \textbf{Volatility targeting} (\S\ref{sec:voltarget}): $\hat w_i^{\text{raw}}$ is
    rescaled so the portfolio's \emph{estimated} annualized volatility matches a target (e.g.\
    20\%), using one of three covariance estimators.
  \item \textbf{RiskLSTM overlay} (\S\ref{sec:risklstm}, \textbf{optional}): frozen
    PortfolioLSTM output is fed, together with rolling per-asset risk statistics and
    (optionally) cross-sectional market-structure statistics, to a second network that
    outputs a per-asset attenuation factor in $[\text{max\_attenuation},1]$.
  \item \textbf{Reporting} (\S\ref{sec:benchmark}): realized PnL, Sharpe ratios, and an
    inverse-volatility benchmark (vol-matched to the model) are computed on all three splits.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{rationale}\rationalelabel
The two networks are trained \emph{sequentially}, not jointly: PortfolioLSTM is trained (or
loaded) to completion first, with \emph{no notion} that a risk overlay might follow;
RiskLSTM is then trained on the frozen, already-decided portfolio purely to improve its
realized Sharpe ratio by learning \emph{when to size it down}. This separation keeps each
network's optimization problem well-posed and interpretable: PortfolioLSTM's gradient
signal is entirely about \emph{which assets to hold}, while RiskLSTM's gradient signal is
entirely about \emph{how much of the already-chosen book to actually take}. A joint
objective would conflate the two and make it impossible to attribute a Sharpe improvement to
either network individually.
\end{rationale}

\subsection{Price loading and log returns}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Log-return conversion.}, label=lst:logret]
def to_log_returns(prices: pd.DataFrame) -> pd.DataFrame:
    """Convert a price DataFrame to log returns: r_t = log(p_t / p_t-1)."""
    return np.log(prices / prices.shift(1)).dropna()
\end{lstlisting}
\srcref{models/portfolio\_lstm.py:189--191}

For close price $p_t$, the log return is
\begin{equation}
  r_t = \log\!\left(\frac{p_t}{p_{t-1}}\right), \qquad r_t \in \mathbb{R}^{N_{\text{real}}}
  \label{eq:logret}
\end{equation}
where $N_{\text{real}}$ is the number of \emph{real} (non-\texttt{CASH}) tradable pairs.
\code{load\_close\_prices} (lines 165--192) fetches an inner-joined (dropna'd), wide
price DataFrame -- one column per pair, indexed by date -- via \code{data/db.py}, downloading
and upserting any missing pair through \code{FXDownloader} on demand.

\begin{rationale}\rationalelabel
Log returns (rather than simple returns) are used because they are additive across time
($\sum_t r_t = \log(p_T/p_0)$) and because standard deviation/variance formulas for
volatility targeting and risk-factor decomposition assume additivity of the underlying
process.
\end{rationale}

\subsection{Feature engineering: carry and vol-normalized momentum}
\label{sec:features}

\begin{optional}\optionallabel
Two additional per-asset feature channels can be enabled independently: FX \textbf{carry}
(\texttt{use\_carry}) and \textbf{vol-normalized momentum} at one or more horizons
(\texttt{vol\_horizons}). Both default to \emph{off} ($C=1$: raw return only).
\end{optional}

\paragraph{Carry.} For a pair with base currency $b$ and quote currency $q$ (e.g.\
\texttt{EURUSD} $\Rightarrow b=$EUR, $q=$USD), carry is the interest-rate differential:
\begin{equation}
  \text{carry}_t^{(b,q)} = \frac{\rho_t^{(b)} - \rho_t^{(q)}}{100}
  \label{eq:carry}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_t^{(c)}$ is currency $c$'s short-term policy rate (percentage points, from FRED,
monthly frequency) at time $t$, divided by 100 to bring it to the same order of magnitude as
a log return. Reindexed onto the daily returns index with \textbf{forward-fill only}
(never back-filled) so that no future rate print ever leaks into an earlier day; any day
before the first available print is set to a neutral $0$.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Carry construction (forward-fill only -- no look-ahead).}, label=lst:carry]
diff = (rates[base] - rates[quote]) / 100.0   # percentage points -> decimal
carry[pair] = diff.reindex(carry.index, method="ffill").fillna(0.0)
\end{lstlisting}
\srcref{models/portfolio\_lstm.py:207--241 (function \texttt{load\_carry})}

\paragraph{Vol-normalized momentum.} For horizon $h$ (days),
\begin{equation}
  \text{volnorm}_t^{(h)} = \frac{\sum_{k=0}^{h-1} r_{t-k}}{\sigma_h(r)_t + \epsilon}, \qquad
  \sigma_h(r)_t = \mathrm{std}\big(r_{t-h+1},\dots,r_t\big),\quad \epsilon=10^{-8}
  \label{eq:volnorm}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ the trailing $h$-day cumulative return divided by the trailing $h$-day realized
volatility -- a standard risk-adjusted momentum/reversal signal, comparable across assets
and volatility regimes.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Vol-normalized return at horizon $h$.}, label=lst:volnorm]
cum_return = returns.rolling(h).sum()
vol = returns.rolling(h).std() + eps
normalized = cum_return / vol
\end{lstlisting}
\srcref{models/portfolio\_lstm.py:244--265 (function \texttt{vol\_normalized\_returns})}

\paragraph{Asset-major channel layout.} When either optional feature is enabled, $C =
1 + \mathbb{1}[\texttt{use\_carry}] + |\texttt{vol\_horizons}|$, and the feature tensor is
laid out \textbf{asset-major}: all of one asset's $C$ channels are contiguous, then the next
asset's:
\begin{equation}
  \text{features} = \big[\,
    \underbrace{r^{(1)}, \text{carry}^{(1)}, \text{volnorm}^{(1,h_1)}, \dots}_{\text{asset }1,\ C\text{ channels}},\;
    \underbrace{r^{(2)}, \dots}_{\text{asset }2},\; \dots
  \,\big] \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times NC}
  \label{eq:assetmajor}
\end{equation}
with \textbf{channel 0 of every asset's block always the raw return} -- this invariant is
relied on later to recover a raw-returns-only view (channel-stride slicing
$X[:,:,0::C]$) for volatility targeting and for RiskLSTM's input reconstruction, without
carrying a separate copy of the raw series.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Asset-major feature assembly.}, label=lst:buildfeat]
def build_feature_dataframe(returns, pairs, use_carry, vol_horizons, years):
    n_channels = 1 + int(use_carry) + len(vol_horizons)
    ...
    for pair in pairs:
        features[f"{pair}_ret"] = returns[pair]
        if use_carry:
            features[f"{pair}_carry"] = carry[pair]
        for h in vol_horizons:
            features[f"{pair}_vol{h}"] = vol_norm[f"{pair}_vol{h}"]
    features = features.ffill().fillna(0.0)   # never back-filled
    assert features.shape[1] == n_channels * len(pairs)
\end{lstlisting}
\srcref{models/portfolio\_lstm.py:268--306}

\begin{rationale}\rationalelabel
Asset-major (rather than channel-major, i.e.\ all returns then all carries) ordering is
chosen deliberately so that a \emph{contiguous slice} of the feature axis always belongs to
exactly one asset -- this is exactly what the per-asset shared encoder
(\S\ref{sec:perasset}) needs to slice out each asset's own window with a single reshape, and
what the raw-return-recovery stride trick needs.
\end{rationale}

\subsection{Rolling window construction}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Building lookback windows and next-day targets.}, label=lst:seq]
def make_portfolio_sequences(returns, lookback, feature_returns=None):
    values = returns.to_numpy(dtype=np.float32)            # (T, N)
    feature_values = feature_returns.to_numpy(...) if feature_returns is not None else values
    for start in range(max(len(values) - lookback, 0)):
        day_t = start + lookback - 1
        X.append(feature_values[start : day_t + 1])          # (L, N*C)
        next_returns.append(values[day_t + 1])                # (N,)
        dates.append(index[day_t + 1])
\end{lstlisting}
\srcref{models/portfolio\_lstm.py:314--364}

For each decision day $t$ (indexed by sample $i$), the window
\begin{equation}
  X_i = \big[\,\text{features}_{t-L+1},\ \dots,\ \text{features}_{t}\,\big] \in \mathbb{R}^{L\times NC}
\end{equation}
is the model's input, and the realized \emph{next-day} return $r_{t+1}\in\mathbb{R}^N$ (raw,
un-featurized -- always the plain log return, regardless of $C$) is the target used to
compute realized PnL: $\text{pnl}_i = w_i^\top r_{t+1}$.

\begin{dims}\dimslabel
$X \in \mathbb{R}^{B\times L \times NC}$ where $B = T - L$ ($T$ = number of usable days of
history); \code{next\_returns} $\in \mathbb{R}^{B\times N}$.
\end{dims}

\subsection{Standardization}

Per-feature mean/std are fit \textbf{only on the training split} and reused, unchanged, for
validation and test (and, when loading a saved model, restored from its checkpoint rather
than refit):
\begin{equation}
  \mu = \mathrm{mean}_{(B,L)}\big(X^{\text{train,raw}}\big),\qquad
  \sigma = \mathrm{std}_{(B,L)}\big(X^{\text{train,raw}}\big) + 10^{-8},\qquad
  \mu,\sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{NC}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  X^{\text{split}} = \frac{X^{\text{split,raw}} - \mu}{\sigma}, \qquad \text{split}\in\{\text{train},\text{val},\text{test}\}
\end{equation}
\srcref{models/portfolio\_lstm.py:2150--2158 (in \texttt{\_prepare\_data})}

\begin{rationale}\rationalelabel
Standardizing with train-only statistics (never refit on validation/test) prevents any
information about the held-out splits' scale from leaking into what the network sees as
``normal.'' The $+10^{-8}$ epsilon safely handles a zero-variance channel -- e.g.\ a
constant-return \texttt{CASH} column, or \texttt{carry} for a currency pair FRED has no rate
data for -- without a division-by-zero.
\end{rationale}

\section{Chronological Train / Validation / Test Split}
\label{sec:split}
% =============================================================================

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={3-way chronological split.}, label=lst:split]
test_frac = getattr(args, "test_frac", 0.0) or 0.0
n_total = len(X)
n_test = int(n_total * test_frac)
n_remaining = n_total - n_test
n_train = int(n_remaining * args.train_frac)

X_full_train_raw = X[:n_train]
X_full_val_raw   = X[n_train:n_remaining]
X_full_test_raw  = X[n_remaining:]
\end{lstlisting}
\srcref{models/portfolio\_lstm.py:2131--2148 (\texttt{\_prepare\_data})}

The split is strictly \textbf{chronological}: the most recent $\texttt{test\_frac}$ fraction
of \emph{all} samples is carved off \emph{first} as the test set, and \texttt{train\_frac} is
then applied to whatever remains:
\begin{align}
  n_{\text{test}} &= \lfloor n_{\text{total}} \cdot \texttt{test\_frac} \rfloor \\
  n_{\text{remaining}} &= n_{\text{total}} - n_{\text{test}} \\
  n_{\text{train}} &= \lfloor n_{\text{remaining}} \cdot \texttt{train\_frac} \rfloor \\
  n_{\text{val}} &= n_{\text{remaining}} - n_{\text{train}}
\end{align}
With defaults $\texttt{train\_frac}=0.8,\ \texttt{test\_frac}=0.1$: the oldest 72\% of history
is train, the next 18\% is validation, and the most recent 10\% is test.
$\texttt{test\_frac}=0$ recovers the original 2-way split exactly ($n_{\text{test}}=0$).

\begin{rationale}\rationalelabel
\textbf{The test set is held out from every training and model-selection decision} --
unlike validation, whose Sharpe ratio \emph{does} drive best-epoch checkpoint selection
(\S\ref{sec:trainloop}), the test split's returns are computed every epoch purely for live
\emph{display} and never influence which epoch's weights get kept. This is what makes test
performance a genuinely unbiased read on generalization: a model that overfits validation by
sheer trial-and-error across many configurations would still be caught by test, since test
was never available to that selection process at all. Carving test off \emph{before}
train/val (rather than, say, taking a random subset) preserves the temporal ordering assumed
by a real backtest -- the model never trains on data from \emph{after} the days it is
ultimately evaluated on.
\end{rationale}

\section{PortfolioLSTM}
\label{sec:portfolio}
% =============================================================================

PortfolioLSTM maps an input window $X_i \in \mathbb{R}^{L\times NC}$ to a portfolio weight
vector $\hat w_i \in \mathbb{R}^N$. Two encoder architectures are available
(\texttt{encoder\_type}), sharing everything downstream of the encoder (temporal pooling,
weight scheme, previous-weight feedback).

\subsection{Encoder A: concatenated input}
\label{sec:concat}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Concatenated-input encoder (default).}, label=lst:concat]
self.lstm = nn.LSTM(input_size=n_assets * n_channels, hidden_size=hidden_size,
                     num_layers=num_layers, batch_first=True)
head_input_size = hidden_size + n_assets if use_prev_weight else hidden_size
self.head = NoisyLinear(head_input_size, n_assets) if noisy_head else nn.Linear(head_input_size, n_assets)
\end{lstlisting}
\srcref{models/portfolio\_lstm.py:592--607 (\texttt{PortfolioLSTM.\_\_init\_\_})}

One LSTM consumes every asset's features concatenated into a single per-timestep vector:
\begin{equation}
  \text{LSTM}: \mathbb{R}^{B\times L \times NC} \to \big(\text{lstm\_out}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times L\times H},\ h_n \in \mathbb{R}^{1\times B\times H}\big)
\end{equation}
followed by pooling over time (\S\ref{sec:pooling}) to a single $\mathbb{R}^{B\times H}$
summary, then the head (a single \code{Linear}/\code{NoisyLinear} layer):
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\text{logits}}_i = \text{Head}\big([\,\text{pooled}_i \,;\, w_{i-1}\,]\big) \in \mathbb{R}^N
  \qquad (\text{concatenation omitted when } \texttt{use\_prev\_weight}=\text{False})
\end{equation}

\begin{rationale}\rationalelabel
This is the simplest, fastest architecture and the historical default. Its weakness: every
learned weight is tied to a \emph{fixed asset order and count} -- the LSTM's input weight
matrix has one column block per specific asset, and the head's output has one fixed neuron
per specific asset. Changing the universe (adding/removing/reordering pairs) requires
retraining from scratch; this motivated the per-asset encoder below.
\end{rationale}

\subsection{Encoder B: shared per-asset encoder with cross-asset attention}
\label{sec:perasset}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Per-asset encoder + cross-asset combiner.}, label=lst:perasset]
self.asset_lstm = nn.LSTM(input_size=n_channels, hidden_size=hidden_size,
                           num_layers=num_layers, batch_first=True)
if asset_combiner == "attention":
    self.asset_attn = nn.MultiheadAttention(embed_dim=hidden_size, num_heads=n_attn_heads, batch_first=True)
head_input_size = 2 * hidden_size + (1 if use_prev_weight else 0)
self.head = NoisyLinear(head_input_size, 1) if noisy_head else nn.Linear(head_input_size, 1)
\end{lstlisting}
\srcref{models/portfolio\_lstm.py:608--633}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Per-asset encoder forward pass.}, label=lst:perassetfwd]
batch, lookback, _ = x.shape
x_per_asset = x.view(batch, lookback, self.n_assets, self.n_channels).permute(0, 2, 1, 3)
x_flat = x_per_asset.reshape(batch * self.n_assets, lookback, self.n_channels)
lstm_out, (h_n, _) = self.asset_lstm(x_flat)
pooled = self.temporal_pool(lstm_out) if self.pooling == "attention" else h_n[-1]
h = self.dropout(pooled).view(batch, self.n_assets, self.hidden_size)          # (B, N, H)

    context, _ = self.asset_attn(h, h, h, need_weights=False)                  # (B, N, H)

if self.use_prev_weight:
    combined = torch.cat([combined, prev_weight.unsqueeze(-1)], dim=-1)        # (B, N, 2H+1)
logits = self.head(combined).squeeze(-1)                                       # (B, N)
\end{lstlisting}
\srcref{models/portfolio\_lstm.py:669--693}

\textbf{One LSTM, with parameters shared across every asset}, is applied independently to
each asset's own $C$-channel window, by folding the asset dimension into the batch
dimension:
\begin{equation}
  X_i \in \mathbb{R}^{L\times NC} \;\to\; \tilde X_i \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times L\times C}
  \;\to\; \tilde X_i^{\text{flat}} \in \mathbb{R}^{(BN)\times L\times C}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \text{LSTM}_{\text{shared}}: \mathbb{R}^{(BN)\times L\times C} \to h \in \mathbb{R}^{B\times N\times H}
  \qquad(\text{after pooling and reshape})
\end{equation}
Every asset's own hidden state $h^{(k)}_i \in \mathbb{R}^H$ then either \textbf{attends to}
or is \textbf{mean-pooled with} every other asset's hidden state, producing a cross-asset
context $c^{(k)}_i \in \mathbb{R}^H$ (\texttt{asset\_combiner}):
\begin{align}
  \text{attention: } &\;\; c_i = \text{MultiheadAttention}(Q\!=\!h_i, K\!=\!h_i, V\!=\!h_i) \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times H} \label{eq:crossattn}\\
  \text{mean: } &\;\; c^{(k)}_i = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^N h^{(j)}_i \quad \forall k \label{eq:crossmean}
\end{align}
A \textbf{single, shared} per-asset head then maps each asset's own
$[\,h^{(k)}_i \,;\, c^{(k)}_i\,]\in\mathbb{R}^{2H}$ (optionally $+1$ for
$w_{i-1}^{(k)}$, that asset's \emph{own} previous weight, a scalar) to \emph{one} logit:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\text{logit}}_i^{(k)} = \text{Head}_{\text{shared}}\big([\,h^{(k)}_i\,;\,c^{(k)}_i\,(;\,w_{i-1}^{(k)})\,]\big) \in \mathbb{R}
\end{equation}
stacked over $k=1,\dots,N$ to form $\hat{\text{logits}}_i \in \mathbb{R}^N$, exactly as in
the concatenated encoder.

\begin{rationale}\rationalelabel
Because \emph{every} learned weight (the shared LSTM, the attention/mean combiner, the
shared head) is reused across assets rather than tied to one specific asset index, this
architecture is \textbf{permutation-equivariant}: reordering the input pairs reorders the
output weights identically (verified empirically -- \S\ref{sec:verification}). It is also,
in principle, \textbf{universe-size invariant}: nothing in the forward pass depends on a
fixed value of $N$ except bookkeeping (reshape targets, zero-initialization width), so the
same trained weights could in principle score a different \emph{number} of assets than
trained on (not currently exposed as a supported workflow, but architecturally unconstrained).
Self-attention across assets additionally lets the network learn genuine \emph{pairwise}
relationships (e.g.\ two correlated pairs, or a natural hedge) rather than only a
symmetric market-wide average, which is what the simpler \texttt{"mean"} combiner provides
as a cheaper, more stable alternative when there are few assets or little training history.
\end{rationale}

\begin{dims}\dimslabel
$x \in \mathbb{R}^{B\times L\times NC} \to$ view $\to (B,L,N,C) \to$ permute $\to (B,N,L,C)
\to$ reshape $\to (BN, L, C)$; asset-LSTM output pooled to $(BN,H) \to$ view $\to (B,N,H)$;
context $(B,N,H)$; \code{combined} $(B,N,2H)$ or $(B,N,2H{+}1)$; \code{logits} after
\code{squeeze(-1)}: $(B,N)$.
\end{dims}

\subsection{Temporal pooling: last hidden state vs.\ attention pooling}
\label{sec:pooling}

\begin{optional}\optionallabel
\texttt{pooling="last"} (default) uses $h_n[-1]$, the LSTM's final hidden state, as the whole
window's summary. \texttt{pooling="attention"} instead uses a learned attention pool over
\emph{every} timestep's hidden state. Applies identically to both PortfolioLSTM encoders and
to RiskLSTM.
\end{optional}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Learned attention pooling over the full LSTM output sequence.}, label=lst:temporalpool]
class TemporalAttentionPool(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, hidden_size):
        self.score = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(hidden_size, hidden_size), nn.Tanh(),
                                    nn.Linear(hidden_size, 1))
    def forward(self, lstm_out):                    # (*, T, H) -> (*, H)
        scores = self.score(lstm_out)                # (*, T, 1)
        weights = torch.softmax(scores, dim=-2)       # (*, T, 1)
        return (weights * lstm_out).sum(dim=-2)       # (*, H)
\end{lstlisting}
\srcref{models/portfolio\_lstm.py:458--492}

For an LSTM output sequence $h_1,\dots,h_T \in \mathbb{R}^H$ (the standard additive/Bahdanau
attention pool):
\begin{equation}
  s_t = v^\top \tanh(W h_t), \qquad \alpha_t = \frac{\exp(s_t)}{\sum_{\tau=1}^T \exp(s_\tau)},
  \qquad \text{pooled} = \sum_{t=1}^T \alpha_t\, h_t
  \label{eq:attnpool}
\end{equation}
where $W\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times H}$, $v \in \mathbb{R}^{H}$ are learned (\code{self.score}'s
two \code{Linear} layers). The leading dimension is generic: for PortfolioLSTM's per-asset
encoder, pooling is applied to a $(BN, T, H)$ tensor (each asset-sample row pooled
independently); for the concatenated encoder and for RiskLSTM, to a $(B,T,H)$ tensor.

\begin{rationale}\rationalelabel
$h_n[-1]$ forces the LSTM to compress everything relevant about the whole window into
whatever it happens to be carrying at the very \emph{last} timestep -- a real limitation for
longer lookbacks where the most decision-relevant days (e.g.\ a vol spike) might sit in the
middle rather than at the end. Attention pooling instead lets the network learn, per
timestep, how relevant that day's hidden state is to the current decision, and combines all
of them into one summary vector.
\end{rationale}

\subsection{Weight schemes}
\label{sec:weightschemes}

\begin{equation}
  \text{softmax (long-only): } \quad \hat w_i = \mathrm{softmax}(\text{logits}_i),
  \quad \hat w_i \succeq 0,\ \; \mathbf{1}^\top \hat w_i = 1
  \label{eq:softmax}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \text{tanh\_norm (long/short): } \quad
  \tilde w_i = \tanh(\text{logits}_i), \quad
  \hat w_i = \frac{\tilde w_i}{\lVert \tilde w_i \rVert_1 + \epsilon}, \quad
  \lVert \hat w_i \rVert_1 = 1
  \label{eq:tanhnorm}
\end{equation}
\srcref{models/portfolio\_lstm.py:635--642 (\texttt{\_weights\_from\_logits})}

\begin{rationale}\rationalelabel
\texttt{softmax} guarantees a valid long-only book (non-negative, fully invested) by
construction -- convenient when short positions are undesirable or unavailable.
\texttt{tanh\_norm} allows each asset's weight to be positive \emph{or} negative (a genuine
long/short book), normalized so the book's gross exposure ($\sum_k |\hat w_i^{(k)}|$) is
exactly 1, matching softmax's ``fully invested'' property in gross-exposure terms while
allowing sign flexibility.
\end{rationale}

\subsection{Recurrent-policy feedback: feeding the previous weight into the head}
\label{sec:prevweight}

\begin{optional}\optionallabel
\texttt{use\_prev\_weight} (default \texttt{False}) concatenates the previously realized
weight $w_{i-1}$ into the head's input, following the classic Moody \& Saffell
recurrent-policy trick, so the network can reason about whether a rebalance is worth its
transaction cost rather than deciding each window as if starting from flat.
\end{optional}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={The sequential recurrent-policy loop (\texttt{forward\_sequence}).}, label=lst:fseq]
def forward_sequence(self, X, initial_weight=None):
    if not self.use_prev_weight:
        return self.forward(X)              # ordinary vectorized batch call

    n = X.shape[0]
    prev = initial_weight.detach() if initial_weight is not None else torch.zeros(self.n_assets, ...)
    freeze = self.noisy_head and self.training
    if freeze:
        self.head.resample_noise(); self.head.freeze_noise(True)
    try:
        weights = []
        for i in range(n):
            w = self.forward(X[i:i+1], prev.unsqueeze(0)).squeeze(0)
            weights.append(w)
            prev = w.detach()                # DETACHED - not full BPTT
        return torch.stack(weights, dim=0)
    finally:
        if freeze: self.head.freeze_noise(False)
\end{lstlisting}
\srcref{models/portfolio\_lstm.py:695--755}

When \texttt{use\_prev\_weight=False}, sample $i$'s decision is independent of sample
$i{-}1$'s, so $B$ samples are processed in one ordinary vectorized batch call -- identical
speed to a model without this feature. When \texttt{use\_prev\_weight=True}, sample $i$'s
\emph{input} genuinely depends on sample $i{-}1$'s \emph{output}, forcing a sequential
Python loop over $i=1,\dots,B$:
\begin{equation}
  w_i = f_\theta(X_i,\, w_{i-1}), \qquad w_{i-1} \leftarrow \text{sg}(w_{i-1})
  \label{eq:recurrentpolicy}
\end{equation}
where $\text{sg}(\cdot)$ (``stop-gradient'', i.e.\ \code{.detach()}) means $w_{i-1}$ is
\textbf{detached} before being fed forward as input to step $i$: gradients flow normally
through \emph{each step's own local computation} (all steps share the same LSTM/head
parameters $\theta$, and a loss summed over the whole path trains $\theta$ using every
step's signal), but this is \textbf{not} full backpropagation-through-time -- unrolling
gradients through hundreds or thousands of sequential steps would be both computationally
intractable and numerically unstable. This is a deliberate simplification: the network
observes its own last action as part of its state (a genuine recurrent policy), but is not
asked to reason about how today's decision causally shapes next week's gradient.

\begin{fixnote}\fixnotelabel
\textbf{NoisyLinear in-place-mutation fix.} \code{NoisyLinear} resamples its noise buffers
(\code{weight\_epsilon}, \code{bias\_epsilon}) \emph{in place} on every \code{forward()} call
while training (\S\ref{sec:noisynet}). The sequential loop above calls
\code{self.forward()} once \emph{per day} in the training period -- without intervention,
every one of those $B$ calls would overwrite the \emph{same} buffer in place, corrupting an
earlier step's saved value before the eventual single \code{loss.backward()} over the whole
period could use it (a real \code{RuntimeError}: \emph{``one of the variables needed for
gradient computation has been modified by an in-place operation''}). The fix:
\code{resample\_noise()} is called \textbf{once} before the loop (one fresh noise draw for
this whole sequential pass, matching what one ordinary \code{forward()} call would get), then
\code{freeze\_noise(True)} holds it fixed for the loop's duration, and
\code{freeze\_noise(False)} in a \code{finally} block restores normal per-call resampling
afterward. \textbf{Design constraint this reveals:} enabling \texttt{noisy\_head} together
with \texttt{use\_prev\_weight} forces one shared noise draw per training epoch (rather than
per day) -- a strictly coarser noise schedule than the non-recurrent path, but the only one
compatible with a single backward pass over the whole trajectory.
\end{fixnote}

\begin{rationale}\rationalelabel
\code{initial\_weight} lets a caller continue a position \emph{across} a split boundary
(e.g.\ validation continues from training's last decided weight, test continues from
validation's) rather than restarting from flat at each boundary -- a real portfolio does not
reset to cash at the end of a backtest period.
\end{rationale}

\subsection{NoisyNet: parameter-space exploration noise}
\label{sec:noisynet}

\begin{optional}\optionallabel
\texttt{noisy\_head} (default \texttt{False}) replaces the head's plain \code{nn.Linear}
with \code{NoisyLinear} (Fortunato et al., 2017), whose weight/bias are themselves stochastic.
\end{optional}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={NoisyLinear: independent Gaussian parameter noise.}, label=lst:noisy]
class NoisyLinear(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, in_features, out_features, sigma_init=0.5):
        self.weight_mu    = nn.Parameter(torch.empty(out_features, in_features))
        self.weight_sigma = nn.Parameter(torch.empty(out_features, in_features))
        self.bias_mu      = nn.Parameter(torch.empty(out_features))
        self.bias_sigma   = nn.Parameter(torch.empty(out_features))
        self.register_buffer("weight_epsilon", torch.empty(out_features, in_features))
        self.register_buffer("bias_epsilon",   torch.empty(out_features))

    def forward(self, x):
        if self.training:
            if not self._frozen: self._reset_noise()     # epsilon ~ N(0,1), in place
            weight = self.weight_mu + self.weight_sigma * self.weight_epsilon
            bias   = self.bias_mu   + self.bias_sigma   * self.bias_epsilon
        else:
            weight, bias = self.weight_mu, self.bias_mu   # deterministic at inference
        return F.linear(x, weight, bias)
\end{lstlisting}
\srcref{models/portfolio\_lstm.py:371--455}

\begin{equation}
  W = \mu_W + \sigma_W \odot \varepsilon_W,\quad \varepsilon_W \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)^{d_{\text{out}}\times d_{\text{in}}},
  \qquad
  b = \mu_b + \sigma_b \odot \varepsilon_b,\quad \varepsilon_b \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)^{d_{\text{out}}}
\end{equation}
with $\sigma_W,\sigma_b$ initialized to $\texttt{sigma\_init}/\sqrt{d_{\text{in}}}$
(\texttt{sigma\_init}$=0.5$ default) and \emph{learned} via gradient descent like any other
parameter, so the model can shrink the noise magnitude over training if it stops helping. At
inference (\code{model.eval()}), $\mu$ alone is used -- deterministic, mirroring how dropout
also disables itself outside training.

\begin{rationale}\rationalelabel
This is the ``noisy cell'' analogue of \code{noise\_std} (which perturbs the \emph{input}
window instead, \S\ref{sec:trainloop}): rather than the model seeing a slightly different
input each epoch, it must produce a good Sharpe ratio under a slightly different version of
its \emph{own decision boundary} each epoch, making it harder to lock onto one razor-sharp
weight configuration that happens to maximize Sharpe on the exact training sequence but does
not generalize. The two noise sources compose (both may be enabled simultaneously) rather
than substitute for each other. \code{RiskLSTM} applies the \emph{same} noisy-linear
treatment to \textbf{both} of its head layers (\S\ref{sec:risklstm}), for full NoisyNet
coverage of its decision path.
\end{rationale}

\section{Volatility Targeting}
\label{sec:voltarget}

PortfolioLSTM's raw weights $\hat w_i^{\text{raw}}$ are rescaled so the portfolio's
\emph{estimated} annualized volatility matches a target (e.g.\ 20\%):
\begin{equation}
  \hat\sigma_i = \sqrt{\hat w_i^{\text{raw}\top} \hat\Sigma_i\, \hat w_i^{\text{raw}}} \cdot \sqrt{252},
  \qquad
  \hat w_i = \hat w_i^{\text{raw}} \cdot \min\!\left(\frac{\texttt{target\_vol}}{\max(\hat\sigma_i, 10^{-8})},\ \texttt{max\_leverage}\right)
  \label{eq:voltarget}
\end{equation}
\srcref{models/portfolio\_lstm.py:1384--1453 (\texttt{portfolio\_volatility}, \texttt{scale\_weights\_to\_target\_vol})}

\begin{dims}\dimslabel
$\hat\Sigma_i \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times N}$ per sample; $\hat w_i^{\text{raw}} \in \mathbb{R}^N$;
$\hat\sigma_i \in \mathbb{R}$ (scalar per sample); batched over $B$ samples via
\code{torch.einsum}.
\end{dims}

\subsection{Three covariance estimators}

\begin{optional}\optionallabel
\texttt{covariance\_estimator} (default \texttt{"sample"}) selects among three estimators of
$\hat\Sigma_i$ from the same-sample raw-return window $X_i^{\text{raw}} \in \mathbb{R}^{L\times N}$
(channel-0-only, i.e.\ raw returns, never carry or vol-normalized channels).
\end{optional}

\paragraph{(a) Sample covariance (default).}
\begin{equation}
  \bar r_i = \frac{1}{L}\sum_{t=1}^{L} X_i^{(t)}, \qquad
  \hat\Sigma_i^{\text{sample}} = \frac{1}{L-1}\sum_{t=1}^{L} (X_i^{(t)} - \bar r_i)(X_i^{(t)} - \bar r_i)^\top
  \label{eq:samplecov}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{(b) EWMA (RiskMetrics-style).} Day $t$ ($t{=}0$ oldest, $t{=}L{-}1$ newest) is
weighted $\propto \lambda^{L-1-t}$, renormalized to sum to 1 (a finite window truncates the
infinite geometric series):
\begin{equation}
  \omega_t = (1-\lambda)\lambda^{L-1-t} \Big/ \sum_{\tau} (1-\lambda)\lambda^{L-1-\tau},
  \qquad
  \bar r_i^{\text{ewma}} = \sum_t \omega_t X_i^{(t)}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \hat\Sigma_i^{\text{ewma}} = \sum_{t=1}^L \omega_t\,(X_i^{(t)} - \bar r_i^{\text{ewma}})(X_i^{(t)} - \bar r_i^{\text{ewma}})^\top
  \label{eq:ewmacov}
\end{equation}
with $\lambda = \texttt{ewma\_lambda}$ (default $0.94$, the RiskMetrics daily default,
$\approx$11-day half-life).

\paragraph{(c) Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage (Ledoit \& Wolf, 2004).} Shrinks the sample covariance
toward a well-conditioned scaled-identity target $F = \bar\mu I$, $\bar\mu = \frac{1}{N}\mathrm{tr}(\hat\Sigma^{\text{sample}})$,
using the analytically optimal (not fixed/hand-tuned) shrinkage intensity:
\begin{align}
  \hat\pi &= \frac{1}{L-1}\sum_{t=1}^L \big\lVert X_i^{(t)}X_i^{(t)\top} - \hat\Sigma_i^{\text{sample}} \big\rVert_F^2
    \quad \text{(estimator noise)} \\
  \hat\gamma &= \big\lVert \hat\Sigma_i^{\text{sample}} - F \big\rVert_F^2 \quad \text{(target misspecification)} \\
  \delta^\ast &= \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\frac{\hat\pi}{\max(\hat\gamma, 10^{-12})},\, 0,\, 1\right) \\
  \hat\Sigma_i^{\text{LW}} &= \delta^\ast F + (1-\delta^\ast)\, \hat\Sigma_i^{\text{sample}}
  \label{eq:ledoitwolf}
\end{align}
\srcref{models/portfolio\_lstm.py:1297--1381 (\texttt{estimate\_covariance})}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage intensity (batched, vectorized).}, label=lst:lw]
outer = torch.einsum("bti,btj->btij", centered, centered)            # per-day outer products
deviation = outer - sample_cov.unsqueeze(1)
pi_hat = (deviation ** 2).sum(dim=(-1, -2)).mean(dim=1) / max(lookback - 1, 1)
gamma_hat = ((sample_cov - target) ** 2).sum(dim=(-1, -2))
delta = (pi_hat / gamma_hat.clamp(min=1e-12)).clamp(min=0.0, max=1.0)
return delta * target + (1.0 - delta) * sample_cov
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{rationale}\rationalelabel
The plain sample covariance over a short window ($L{=}30$ days by default) is noisy: a
handful of coincidentally calm or coincidentally correlated days can swing the whole
estimate, and if $L \le N$ it can even be exactly singular. EWMA is a strictly cheaper
improvement -- weighting recent days more lets the estimate react faster to a genuine
volatility regime change while still using the whole window for stability. Ledoit-Wolf goes
further: it inflates small eigenvalues toward a well-conditioned target using an intensity
\emph{derived}, not guessed, from how noisy the sample estimator itself is -- directly
addressing the sample estimator's worst failure mode for vol targeting (see
\texttt{max\_leverage} below): a near-singular covariance produces a spuriously tiny
estimated risk and hence an enormous leverage scale-up, right before a normal-sized move can
wipe out the position. Empirically verified (unit test): on a rank-1 (perfectly collinear)
synthetic window, the sample estimator's smallest eigenvalue is $\approx 0$ while
Ledoit-Wolf's is safely bounded away from zero.
\end{rationale}

\subsection{The leverage cap}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={\texttt{max\_leverage} caps the scale factor itself.}, label=lst:maxlev]
if target_vol == 0: return weights
vol = portfolio_volatility(window_returns, weights, ...).clamp(min=1e-8)
scale = (target_vol / vol).clamp(max=max_leverage)
return weights * scale.unsqueeze(-1)
\end{lstlisting}
\srcref{models/portfolio\_lstm.py:1409--1453}

\begin{rationale}\rationalelabel
Without a cap on \emph{the scale factor itself} (not merely a numerical-stability clamp
buried inside the volatility estimate), a window whose ESTIMATED covariance happens to be
near-zero can be scaled up by $1000\times$ or more (confirmed empirically on realistic
synthetic data), and that single sample can then realize a catastrophic ($<{-}100\%$)
return the moment the actual next-day move is not equally calm. This is especially severe for
the \texttt{"kelly"} training objective: $\log(1+r)$ has a singularity at $r={-}1$, and the
safety clamp used there produces an \emph{exact zero gradient} for any return at or below it
-- the model would get no corrective signal from precisely the sample that most needs one.
Capping leverage at a sane multiple (default $10\times$) bounds every sample's worst-case
return and fixes this dead-gradient failure mode \emph{at its source}, rather than papering
over the symptom independently in each loss function.
\end{rationale}

\section{Training Objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}
% =============================================================================

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Objective dispatcher.}, label=lst:dispatch]
def compute_training_loss(portfolio_returns, objective, sharpe_window=60, cvar_alpha=0.95, cvar_kappa=1.0):
    if objective == "sharpe": return -rolling_window_ratio(portfolio_returns, window=sharpe_window, downside=True)
    if objective == "kelly":  return kelly_loss(portfolio_returns)
    if objective == "cvar":   return mean_cvar_loss(portfolio_returns, alpha=cvar_alpha, kappa=cvar_kappa)
\end{lstlisting}
\srcref{models/portfolio\_lstm.py:1264--1290}

\subsection{Rolling-window Sortino-style ratio (\texttt{"sharpe"}, default)}

\begin{equation}
  \text{ratio}_j = \frac{\overline{r}_j}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{w}\sum_{t\in W_j} \min(r_t,0)^2} + \epsilon} \cdot \sqrt{252},
  \qquad
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{sharpe}} = -\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^J \text{ratio}_j
  \label{eq:sortino}
\end{equation}
where $W_j$ ranges over all $J = B - w + 1$ overlapping $w$-day sub-windows
(\texttt{sharpe\_window}, default 60) of the $B$-day training period, and the denominator is
a \textbf{downside} deviation (only negative returns penalized -- the Sortino variant) rather
than the plain standard deviation.
\srcref{models/portfolio\_lstm.py:1131--1177 (\texttt{rolling\_window\_ratio})}

\begin{rationale}\rationalelabel
Scoring the mean ratio over many overlapping sub-windows, rather than one ratio over the
whole training period, prevents the model from finding one weight pattern that happens to
nail the exact historical sequence end-to-end while behaving arbitrarily badly on any
sub-period. Downside-only risk (Sortino) does not penalize \emph{upside} volatility, which
is a more appropriate risk definition for a return-seeking strategy than symmetric variance.
\end{rationale}

\subsection{Kelly criterion (\texttt{"kelly"})}

\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{kelly}} = -\mathbb{E}\big[\log(1+r_t)\big] \approx -\frac{1}{B}\sum_{t=1}^B \log\big(\max(1+r_t,\epsilon)\big)
  \label{eq:kelly}
\end{equation}
\srcref{models/portfolio\_lstm.py:1180--1202}

\begin{rationale}\rationalelabel
Maximizes expected \emph{log}-wealth growth -- the Kelly criterion -- which is convex in the
portfolio weights (no extra tunable risk-aversion weight needed) and targets long-run
compounded growth rather than a single-period ratio. Its numerical fragility at $r\to -1$ is
exactly why \texttt{max\_leverage} (\S\ref{sec:voltarget}) exists.
\end{rationale}

\subsection{Mean-CVaR (\texttt{"cvar"})}

\begin{equation}
  \text{CVaR}_\alpha = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k} \ell_{(j)}, \qquad \ell_t = -r_t,\quad
  k = \max\big(1, \mathrm{round}((1-\alpha) B)\big)
  \label{eq:cvar}
\end{equation}
(mean of the $k$ worst realized losses -- $\ell_{(1)}\ge\dots\ge\ell_{(k)}$ are the top-$k$
losses via \code{torch.topk}, whose gradient flows through whichever days are currently the
worst).
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{cvar}} = -\underbrace{\Big(\tfrac{1}{B}\textstyle\sum_t r_t\Big)\cdot 252}_{\text{annualized mean}}
  \; + \; \kappa \cdot \underbrace{\text{CVaR}_\alpha \cdot \sqrt{252}}_{\text{annualized CVaR}}
  \label{eq:meancvar}
\end{equation}
\srcref{models/portfolio\_lstm.py:1205--1261 (\texttt{cvar}, \texttt{mean\_cvar\_loss})}

\begin{rationale}\rationalelabel
This is the textbook convex portfolio objective (Rockafellar \& Uryasev, 2000): both the
negative mean (affine) and CVaR (convex) are convex in the weights, so their nonnegative
combination is convex in weight-space -- at the cost of \texttt{cvar\_kappa}, a
\emph{fixed} (not learned) risk-aversion weight, since there is no way to combine two
different quantities into one strictly convex scalar without such a weight (it plays the
same structural role as mean-variance's $\lambda$, applied to CVaR instead of variance).
\textbf{Both terms are annualized before combining} -- this was a deliberate fix: on raw
daily FX returns the mean is tiny while CVaR (average of the worst days) is 10--30$\times$
larger in practice, so combining them \emph{unannualized} with $\kappa{=}1.0$ made the loss
almost entirely about minimizing CVaR, with the mean term contributing well under 10\% of
the gradient signal -- the model barely learned to seek return at all. Annualizing first
(mean $\times 252$, CVaR $\times\sqrt{252}$, matching how Sharpe itself annualizes) brings
both terms to a comparable order of magnitude, making $\kappa{=}1.0$ a genuinely balanced
default.
\end{rationale}

\section{Transaction Costs}
\label{sec:txcost}
% =============================================================================

\begin{equation}
  \text{turnover}_t = \begin{cases} \lVert w_0 \rVert_1 & t=0 \\ \lVert w_t - w_{t-1} \rVert_1 & t>0 \end{cases},
  \qquad
  r_t^{\text{net}} = r_t - \frac{\texttt{transaction\_cost\_bps}}{10^4}\cdot \text{turnover}_t
  \label{eq:txcost}
\end{equation}
\srcref{models/portfolio\_lstm.py:1514--1573 (\texttt{apply\_transaction\_costs}, and the differentiable \texttt{apply\_transaction\_costs\_torch})}

\begin{rationale}\rationalelabel
Costs are applied \textbf{net, inside the training loss itself} (via the differentiable
\code{apply\_transaction\_costs\_torch}), not only as a post-hoc reporting adjustment -- so
the model is trained to maximize its objective \emph{net of} trading costs directly, learning
to avoid abrupt, expensive day-to-day weight swings whenever they are not worth their cost
(this is precisely what motivates \texttt{use\_prev\_weight}, \S\ref{sec:prevweight}: without
seeing its own previous position, the model cannot reason about whether a rebalance is
worth its cost at all). The day-0 cost ($\lVert w_0\rVert_1$) charges the cost of putting the
initial position on from flat.
\end{rationale}

# after the loop:
if track_val and best_state is not None:
    model.load_state_dict(best_state)          # restore best-VALIDATION-Sharpe epoch
\end{lstlisting}
\srcref{models/portfolio\_lstm.py:1580--1744}

Training is \textbf{full-batch}: every epoch computes weights for the \emph{entire} training
period at once ($B_{\text{train}}$ samples in parallel, or sequentially if
\texttt{use\_prev\_weight}), because Sharpe/Sortino/CVaR are statistics of a whole return
\emph{distribution} and cannot be meaningfully evaluated on one sample at a time the way a
per-sample loss (e.g.\ MSE) can.

\begin{rationale}\rationalelabel
\textbf{Checkpoint selection by validation Sharpe} (the plain whole-period metric, \emph{not}
whatever \texttt{objective} is training against) rather than always keeping the last epoch
directly targets an in-sample-great, out-of-sample-poor training curve: the run keeps
whichever epoch's weights actually generalized best, a standard early-stopping /
checkpoint-selection use of a validation split. \textbf{Test-set tracking} (when
\texttt{test\_frac}$>0$) computes the identical quantity on the test split every epoch too,
but \emph{purely for live display} -- it never enters \texttt{best\_state} selection,
preserving test's status as a genuinely unbiased read (\S\ref{sec:split}). \texttt{noise\_std}
perturbs only the \emph{standardized input}, not the clean window used for vol-targeting
(that estimate must reflect real, not artificially noised, risk).
\end{rationale}

\subsection{Marginal risk features: rolling moments of the weighted PnL path}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Rolling std / skewness / excess kurtosis.}, label=lst:moments]
def rolling_moments(x, window, eps=1e-6):                 # x: (B, L, N)
    windows = x.unfold(dimension=1, size=window, step=1)   # (B, T', N, window)
    mean = windows.mean(dim=-1, keepdim=True)
    centered = windows - mean
    variance = (centered ** 2).mean(dim=-1)
    std = torch.sqrt(variance + eps)
    skewness = (centered ** 3).mean(dim=-1) / (std ** 3 + eps)
    kurtosis = (centered ** 4).mean(dim=-1) / (std ** 4 + eps) - 3.0        # excess
    return torch.cat([std, skewness, kurtosis], dim=-1)      # (B, T', 3N)
\end{lstlisting}
\srcref{models/risk\_lstm.py:94--128}

For each asset $k$ and trailing $W$-day sub-window ending at $t$, on the \textbf{per-asset
weighted PnL path} $p_t^{(k)} = \hat w^{(k)}\cdot r_t^{(k)}$ (the raw return path, scaled by
that sample's OWN decision weight -- i.e.\ statistics of the asset's \emph{actual exposure},
which vol-targeting may have leveraged up or \texttt{tanh\_norm} may have flipped short --
rather than of the asset's own unlevered return):
\begin{align}
  \mu_t^{(k)} &= \frac{1}{W}\sum_{\tau=t-W+1}^{t} p_\tau^{(k)}, \qquad
  \sigma_t^{(k)} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{W}\sum_\tau (p_\tau^{(k)}-\mu_t^{(k)})^2 + \epsilon} \\
  \text{skew}_t^{(k)} &= \frac{\frac{1}{W}\sum_\tau (p_\tau^{(k)}-\mu_t^{(k)})^3}{(\sigma_t^{(k)})^3 + \epsilon},
  \qquad
  \text{kurt}_t^{(k)} = \frac{\frac{1}{W}\sum_\tau (p_\tau^{(k)}-\mu_t^{(k)})^4}{(\sigma_t^{(k)})^4 + \epsilon} - 3
  \label{eq:moments}
\end{align}

\begin{rationale}\rationalelabel
Feeding these three moments \emph{directly}, rather than hoping an LSTM rediscovers them from
raw returns, is a more direct and sample-efficient way to teach a small network ``is there a
clear, stable trend or not'': std is realized volatility (the classic risk signal), skewness
flags asymmetric tails (crash risk vs.\ melt-up), and excess kurtosis (0 for a Normal
distribution) flags fat tails / regime instability. Using the \emph{weighted} PnL (not the
raw return) means these statistics reflect the asset's actual current exposure, not its
unlevered volatility -- a highly-levered small position can be riskier in dollar terms than
an unlevered large one.
\end{rationale}

\subsection{Cross-sectional risk features (optional)}
\label{sec:xsec}

\begin{optional}\optionallabel
\texttt{use\_cross\_sectional} (default \texttt{False}) appends 3 \textbf{portfolio-wide}
(not per-asset) channels computed from the rolling cross-asset correlation structure of the
\textbf{raw, unweighted} return path (deliberately not the weighted path -- a zero-weighted
asset's degenerate constant series would make correlation undefined).
\end{optional}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Cross-sectional correlation-structure features.}, label=lst:xsec]
def cross_sectional_features(x, window, eps=1e-6):        # x: (B, T, N) RAW returns
    windows = x.unfold(1, window, 1)                        # (B, T', N, window)
    normalized = (windows - windows.mean(-1,keepdim=True)) / std          # per-asset z-score
    corr = torch.matmul(normalized, normalized.transpose(-1,-2)) / window # (B, T', N, N)

    off_diag = corr * (1 - eye);  n_pairs = N * (N - 1)
    avg_corr = off_diag.sum(dim=(-1,-2)) / n_pairs
    corr_dispersion = sqrt(((corr - avg_corr)**2 * (1-eye)).sum(dim=(-1,-2)) / n_pairs)
    eigenvalues = torch.linalg.eigvalsh(corr)                # ascending, real (symmetric)
    top_eig_share = eigenvalues[..., -1] / eigenvalues.sum(dim=-1)
    return torch.stack([avg_corr, corr_dispersion, top_eig_share], dim=-1)   # (B, T', 3)
\end{lstlisting}
\srcref{models/risk\_lstm.py:131--195}

For the rolling $N\times N$ correlation matrix $\hat\rho_t$ (per-asset $z$-scored returns'
Gram matrix, so diagonal $=1$ by construction):
\begin{align}
  \overline{\rho}_t &= \frac{1}{N(N-1)} \sum_{k\ne j} \hat\rho_t^{(k,j)} \qquad \text{[avg.\ pairwise correlation]} \label{eq:avgcorr}\\
  s_t &= \sqrt{\frac{1}{N(N-1)}\sum_{k\ne j} \big(\hat\rho_t^{(k,j)} - \overline{\rho}_t\big)^2} \qquad\text{[correlation dispersion]} \label{eq:corrdisp}\\
  \phi_t &= \frac{\lambda_{\max}(\hat\rho_t)}{\sum_{m=1}^N \lambda_m(\hat\rho_t)} = \frac{\lambda_{\max}(\hat\rho_t)}{N} \qquad\text{[top eigenvalue share]} \label{eq:topeig}
\end{align}
(the trace of a correlation matrix is always $N$, so the denominator of Eq.~\eqref{eq:topeig}
is exactly $N$; $\phi_t \in [1/N, 1]$: close to $1/N$ means no dominant common factor, close
to 1 means one shared risk driver is moving nearly everything). With $N<2$, pairwise
correlation is undefined and these three channels are returned as zero.

\begin{rationale}\rationalelabel
This is the complement to the marginal moments above: an asset can look individually calm
while the \emph{whole book} is one bad day away from moving together -- a regime the
per-asset statistics alone cannot see. Average correlation and its dispersion distinguish a
uniform one-factor market (high average, low dispersion) from a patchwork one (some pairs
correlated, others not); the top-eigenvalue share is the standard PCA-based ``how much
day-to-day covariation is explained by one common factor'' concentration measure. Computed
on \emph{raw}, not weighted, returns so the correlation structure reflects genuine market
co-movement, independent of the current portfolio's own (possibly zero) weights.
\end{rationale}

\subsection{Building RiskLSTM's input sequences}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={\texttt{make\_risk\_sequences}: decoupling \texttt{rolling\_window} from \texttt{lookback}.}, label=lst:mkrisk]
n_valid = n_samples - rolling_window + 1
full_series = torch.cat([X_raw[0], next_returns_raw], dim=0)             # reconstruct continuous series
extended_len = lookback + rolling_window - 1
extended_windows = full_series.unfold(0, extended_len, 1)[:n_valid]      # (n_valid, extended_len, N)

weighted = extended_windows * weights[:, None, :]                       # this sample's OWN decision weight
moments = rolling_moments(weighted, rolling_window)                     # (n_valid, lookback, 3N)
aligned_returns = weighted[:, rolling_window - 1:, :]                   # (n_valid, lookback, N)
features = torch.cat([aligned_returns, moments], dim=-1)                # (n_valid, lookback, 4N)
if use_cross_sectional:
    features = torch.cat([features, cross_sectional_features(extended_windows, rolling_window)], dim=-1)
\end{lstlisting}
\srcref{models/risk\_lstm.py:198--303}

To let \texttt{rolling\_window} ($W$) be sized \emph{independently} of \texttt{lookback}
($L$), the continuous raw-return series underlying $X^{\text{raw}}$ is first reconstructed
(the first window in full, then exactly the one new day each subsequent sample adds), then,
for each sample, an \textbf{extended} slice of length $L+W-1$ (not just $L$) is taken,
weighted by that sample's own decision weight, and \emph{then} rolled with window $W$ --
producing exactly $L$ output timesteps per sample regardless of how $W$ relates to $L$. The
first $W-1$ samples of a split lack enough leading context and are \textbf{dropped} (a
small, fixed cost, independent of $L$); \texttt{weights}/\texttt{next\_returns} are returned
sliced to match, so every downstream consumer (training targets, reporting, plots) reuses
the identical alignment.

\begin{dims}\dimslabel
Final feature tensor: $(n_{\text{valid}},\, L,\, 4N\ [{+}3])$, where
$n_{\text{valid}} = n_{\text{samples}} - W + 1$.
\end{dims}

\subsection{RiskLSTM architecture and forward pass}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={RiskLSTM.\_\_init\_\_ and forward.}, label=lst:risklstm]
n_feature_channels = 4 * n_assets + (3 if use_cross_sectional else 0)
self.lstm = nn.LSTM(input_size=n_feature_channels, hidden_size=hidden_size, ...)
if noisy_head:
    self.head   = NoisyLinear(hidden_size + n_assets, hidden_size // 2)
    self.head_2 = NoisyLinear(hidden_size // 2, n_assets)
else:
    self.head   = nn.Linear(hidden_size + n_assets, hidden_size // 2)
    self.head_2 = nn.Linear(hidden_size // 2, n_assets)

def forward(self, features, portfolio_weights):
    lstm_out, (h_n, _) = self.lstm(features)
    pooled = self.temporal_pool(lstm_out) if self.pooling == "attention" else h_n[-1]
    hidden = self.dropout(pooled)
    combined = torch.cat([hidden, portfolio_weights], dim=-1)             # (B, H_r + N)
    x = self.leaky_relu(self.head(combined))                              # (B, H_r // 2)
    attenuation_logit = torch.sigmoid(self.head_2(x))                     # (B, N)
    return self.max_attenuation + (1.0 - self.max_attenuation) * attenuation_logit
\end{lstlisting}
\srcref{models/risk\_lstm.py:349--441}

\begin{equation}
  a_i = \texttt{max\_attenuation} + (1-\texttt{max\_attenuation})\cdot \sigma\big(\text{Head}_2(\mathrm{LeakyReLU}(\text{Head}([\text{pooled}_i\,;\,\hat w_i])))\big)
  \label{eq:attenuation}
\end{equation}
one value per asset, $a_i \in [\texttt{max\_attenuation},\,1]^N$ (default floor $0.33$: an
asset is de-risked, \textbf{never fully zeroed out}). The final position is
$w_i^{\text{final}} = \hat w_i \odot a_i$ (elementwise).

\begin{rationale}\rationalelabel
The weight vector re-enters at the \emph{head}, not the LSTM's input, mirroring
PortfolioLSTM's \texttt{use\_prev\_weight} conditioning: the LSTM branch tells the network
whether this asset's \emph{recent behavior} looks stable, while the concatenated weight tells
it \emph{what position is actually being considered} -- both are needed to decide how much to
de-risk. LeakyReLU (not plain ReLU) between \code{head} and \code{head\_2} keeps a small
gradient flowing even for units with a negative pre-activation, avoiding ``dying ReLU'' units
that get stuck early in training and never update again. \texttt{max\_attenuation} is a
\textbf{floor, not a ceiling}: the smooth rescaling $\texttt{max\_att} + (1-\texttt{max\_att})\sigma(\cdot)$
has no dead zone (unlike a hard clamp), and the network can never force a position fully to
zero regardless of how unstable an asset looks -- a deliberate risk limit on the overlay's
own authority.
\end{rationale}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={\texttt{train\_risk\_model}: one epoch.}, label=lst:risktrain]

\section{Inverse-Volatility Benchmark}
\label{sec:benchmark}
% =============================================================================

\begin{equation}
  b_t^{\text{raw}} = \sum_{k=1}^N \frac{1/\sigma_t^{(k)}}{\sum_{j=1}^N 1/\sigma_t^{(j)}}\, r_{t+1}^{(k)},
  \qquad \sigma_t^{(k)} = \mathrm{std}\big(X_t^{(\cdot,k)}\big) + 10^{-8}
  \label{eq:benchmark}
\end{equation}
\srcref{models/portfolio\_lstm.py:1460--1485 (\texttt{inverse\_vol\_benchmark\_returns})}

An un-learned, classic inverse-volatility (``naive risk-parity'') allocator: weight each
asset inversely to its own trailing realized volatility over that day's \emph{same} lookback
window the model itself saw, normalized to sum to 1 -- no vol-targeting leverage, no
transaction-cost awareness.

\begin{equation}
  b_t = b_t^{\text{raw}} \cdot \frac{\widehat{\mathrm{std}}(r^{\text{model}})\cdot\sqrt{252}}{\widehat{\mathrm{std}}(b^{\text{raw}})\cdot\sqrt{252}}
  \label{eq:volmatch}
\end{equation}
\srcref{models/portfolio\_lstm.py:1488--1507 (\texttt{vol\_match\_benchmark})}

rescaled by \textbf{one constant multiplier per split} so its realized annualized volatility
matches the \emph{model's own}, computed \textbf{separately for train, validation, and test}.

\begin{rationale}\rationalelabel
A plain risk-parity book runs at whatever volatility the market happens to produce, while the
model is actively vol-targeted -- comparing their raw PnL would conflate ``did the learned
allocation add value'' with ``did the model just happen to run hotter or colder than the
benchmark this period.'' Rescaling the benchmark to the model's own realized volatility (per
split, since realized volatility differs a lot between train/val/test) isolates the former
question, which is the one an allocation-skill comparison actually needs answered.
\end{rationale}

\section{Ensembling: Multi-Seed Restarts}
\label{sec:ensembling}
% =============================================================================

\begin{optional}\optionallabel
\texttt{n\_seeds} $>1$ (default 1) trains that many independently-seeded restarts of
PortfolioLSTM (and, if enabled, RiskLSTM) on identical data, combined via
\texttt{restart\_strategy}.
\end{optional}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={"best" vs.\ "ensemble" restart combination.}, label=lst:ensemble]
if args.restart_strategy == "best":
    best_idx, best = max(enumerate(results), key=lambda item: float(sharpe_ratio(item[1].returns_val)))
    return best
# "ensemble":
ensemble_model = EnsemblePortfolioLSTM([r.model for r in results])
return evaluate_portfolio_model(ensemble_model, data, ...)
\end{lstlisting}
\srcref{models/portfolio\_lstm.py:2374--2425 (\texttt{run\_pipeline\_multi\_seed})}

\begin{equation}
  \text{"best"}: \quad \text{seed}^\ast = \arg\max_{s} \; \text{SharpeRatio}\big(r^{\text{val}}_s\big)
  \label{eq:beststrat}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \text{"ensemble"}: \quad \hat w_i = \frac{\bar w_i}{\lVert \bar w_i \rVert_1 + \epsilon}, \qquad
  \bar w_i = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^S \hat w_i^{(s)}
  \label{eq:ensemblestrat}
\end{equation}
\srcref{models/portfolio\_lstm.py:918--932 (\texttt{EnsemblePortfolioLSTM.forward})}

\begin{rationale}\rationalelabel
The Sharpe-ratio training objective is non-convex in the LSTM's parameters (LSTM/softmax
nonlinearities), so different random seeds converge to different local optima -- exactly the
problem ensembling addresses. \texttt{"best"} picks the single restart with the highest
validation Sharpe; \texttt{"ensemble"} averages every restart's predicted weights (a simple
average of several points on the simplex already sums to 1 for \texttt{softmax} -- the
renormalization is a no-op there -- but is \emph{not} a no-op for \texttt{tanh\_norm}, where
individual models can disagree on an asset's sign, so re-normalizing restores the same
``fully invested'' invariant each individual model already satisfies). Averaging tends to
cancel out each individual restart's idiosyncratic overfitting to its own local optimum.
\texttt{EnsembleRiskLSTM} is simpler still: attenuation values already live in
$[\texttt{max\_attenuation},1]$, and a plain average of values in that range stays in that
range, so no renormalization is needed there.
\end{rationale}

\section{Summary of Architectural Decisions}
% =============================================================================

\begin{longtable}{@{}p{4.0cm}p{9.2cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Decision} & \textbf{Primary rationale} \\
\midrule
\endfirsthead
\toprule
\textbf{Decision} & \textbf{Primary rationale} \\
\midrule
\endhead
Sequential (not joint) two-network training & Keeps each network's gradient signal
attributable to one well-posed sub-problem (asset selection vs.\ risk sizing). \\
Log returns & Time-additive; matches the variance/covariance formulas used everywhere. \\
Carry \& vol-normalized momentum (optional) & Standard, well-documented FX risk-adjusted
signals, asset-major laid out for slice-ability. \\
Forward-fill only (never back-fill) for carry/vol features & Any back-fill would leak a
later day's value into an earlier row -- a genuine look-ahead bias. \\
Per-asset shared encoder + cross-asset attention (optional) & Permutation-equivariant and,
in principle, universe-size invariant, unlike the concatenated encoder's fixed asset-indexed
weight matrices. \\
Attention pooling over time (optional) & $h_n[-1]$ alone can lose mid-window information for
longer lookbacks; a learned pool lets the network decide which days matter most. \\
\texttt{use\_prev\_weight} recurrent policy & Lets the model reason about whether a
rebalance is worth its transaction cost; implemented as a detached (not full-BPTT) sequential
loop for tractability. \\
NoisyNet parameter noise (optional) & A second, complementary form of regularization (in
weight-space, not input-space) against overfitting one exact historical sequence. \\
NoisyLinear freeze/resample fix & Sequential \texttt{forward\_sequence} would otherwise
mutate shared noise buffers in place mid-graph, corrupting autograd; noise is now drawn once
per sequential pass. \\
\texttt{CASH} as a data-layer pseudo-asset & Reuses every downstream mechanism (vol-targeting,
costs, reporting) with zero special-casing; a constant return has exactly zero variance. \\
Three covariance estimators & Sample covariance is noisy/possibly singular on a short window;
EWMA and Ledoit-Wolf are progressively more robust, principled remedies. \\
\texttt{max\_leverage} cap & Bounds the worst-case realized loss from a spuriously
near-singular covariance estimate; fixes Kelly's dead-gradient failure mode at its source. \\
Rolling-window Sortino, Kelly, and CVaR objectives & Three different, well-understood
risk/return trade-offs (non-convex ratio, convex log-wealth, convex tail-risk penalty). \\
Annualizing both mean-CVaR terms & Prevents CVaR's larger raw magnitude from starving the
mean-return gradient signal. \\
Transaction costs inside the training loss & Trains the model to avoid costly, unnecessary
rebalancing directly, not only as a reporting afterthought. \\
Chronological, test-carved-first 3-way split & Test never influences any training or
model-selection decision -- a genuinely unbiased generalization estimate, unlike validation. \\
Best-val-Sharpe checkpoint selection & Targets the epoch that generalized best, not whichever
the fixed epoch budget happened to land on. \\
Marginal + cross-sectional risk features for RiskLSTM & Per-asset moments see individual
risk; cross-sectional correlation/eigenvalue features see portfolio-wide concentration risk
that marginal statistics alone cannot. \\
Attenuation floor (never fully zero) & A deliberate limit on the overlay's authority -- it can
de-risk, never fully close, a position. \\
Multi-seed restarts \& ensembling & Both training objectives are non-convex; restarts and
averaging mitigate sensitivity to a single unlucky initialization. \\
Inverse-vol benchmark, vol-matched per split & Isolates ``did the learned allocation add
value'' from ``did the model just run hotter/colder than a naive book.'' \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}
